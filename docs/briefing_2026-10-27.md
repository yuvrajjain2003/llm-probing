# Part 1. The setup

## What the project is trying to find out

A reasoning model writes out its thinking step by step before answering. Take that half-written
chain of thought and stop it partway. The model has internal numbers (activations) at that moment.

**The question: do those internal numbers tell you what the model is about to do, beyond what you
could tell by just reading the text it has written so far?**

"What it is about to do" is not one thing, so we measure five:

| target | plain meaning |
|---|---|
| answer entropy | if you let it finish 25 times, how spread out are the final answers |
| P(mode) | what fraction of those 25 land on the single most common answer |
| remaining length | how many more words of thinking before it stops, on average |
| length spread | how much that varies across the 25 |
| commitment | has it already locked onto an answer it will keep |

The first four are continuous numbers, scored with R². The last is yes/no, scored with accuracy.

## How the data was built

- **Dataset:** GPQA-Diamond. Hard multiple-choice science questions, PhD level. 198 of them.
- **Model:** DeepSeek-R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. A 7-billion-parameter reasoning model.
- **Chains:** for each question the model wrote 25 complete reasoning chains.
- **Cut points:** each chain is cut at 10%, 30%, 50%, 70% and 90% of the way through, counted in
  sentences.
- **Labels:** from each cut point the model was restarted 25 times and allowed to finish. The five
  numbers above are measured over those 25 finishes.
- **Features:** the model's hidden state at the last token before the cut. Layer 21 of 28. That is
  a list of 3,584 numbers.

So one row of our data is: one question, one chain, one cut point. About 4,900 rows per cut point.

## What R² means here, in one line

R² is "how much better than always guessing the average". 0 means no better than guessing. 1 means
perfect. Our entropy R² of 0.29 means we explain about 29% of the variation.

**It is not a percentage of questions answered correctly.** If he asks whether 0.29 is good, the
honest answer is: it is clearly above chance and clearly above the text baselines, and it is in the
same range as the one comparable published number (more in Part 5).

## How everything is scored, and why it is done this way

Three rules, applied to every single number in the deck. These matter because they are where most
papers in this area go wrong.

1. **Split by question, never by row.** The 25 chains of one question are very alike. If some land
   in training and some in test, the probe can memorise the question instead of learning anything.
   We use 5 folds, split by question, fixed, identical everywhere so every method is compared on
   exactly the same rows.
2. **Score within each cut point, never pooled.** If you pool all cut points together, you get a
   large number for a boring reason: at 10% a chain has a lot of thinking left, at 90% very little,
   so anything that knows roughly how far along you are scores well. Pooled, the trivial baseline
   "how long is the prefix" gets 0.718 and our probe gets 0.716. Scored properly within each cut
   point, our probe is clearly ahead. **If he asks only one methods question, it will probably be
   this one.**
3. **Error bars on everything, paired.** We resample questions 2,000 times and recompute. "Paired"
   means we compute the difference between two methods on each resample, which is much tighter than
   comparing two separate error bars. Typical interval is about plus or minus 0.05.

## The baselines we compare against

**Framing note, important.** The professor does not rate TF-IDF as a baseline, and he is right that
it is a weak one. The point of running so many baselines was never to keep beating word counts: it
was to find out how strong a baseline exists at all. So lead with the strongest one we found, not
the weakest.

The ladder, weakest to strongest:

| baseline | entropy R² | against our 0.289 |
|---|---|---|
| sentence embedders (worst: Granite) | 0.154 | far behind |
| prefix length alone | 0.247 | behind |
| TF-IDF word counts | 0.237 | behind |
| best sentence embedder (UltraBERT) | 0.260 | behind, not significantly |
| Mistral-7B activations | 0.270 | slightly behind, significant |
| frontier model told the task in words | 0.294 | ties us |
| **OLMo-3-7B activations** | **0.282** | **ties us on all five targets** |

- **The strongest baseline is another model's activations.** OLMo-3-7B, which shares no training
  lineage with our model, ties us on all five targets with differences under 0.008. That is the
  number to lead with.
- **TF-IDF and prefix length are the floor, not the story.** Keep them in the table, do not build a
  slide around them.
- **Prefix length is still worth knowing about**, because it beats us on remaining length at the
  early cut points, and because pooled across depth it ties us overall. That is a methods trap, not
  a baseline claim.

# Part 3. The eight asks we completed

## Ask 1. In-context with a smaller prompt (slide 6)

**What he asked for:** try the in-context setup with a shorter prompt.

**Background:** the in-context setup is the "black box" alternative to probing. Instead of opening
the model up, you take some other model, show it k worked examples (a prefix plus the five measured
numbers), then show it a new prefix and ask it to predict the numbers. If a model could just do this
from reading, you would not need activations at all.

Until now every example in those prompts was a complete prefix, which is long. That capped us at 93
examples before running out of context.

**What we did:** cut each example's reasoning down to its last 2 sentences, keeping the question and
answer choices complete. The cut keeps the end of the reasoning because that is where the cut point
is, so it is the part that matters.

**Result:** examples drop to 8.5% of their length, which lets us fit 400 instead of 93. Shortening
does help a little, but it does not change the conclusion.

| setting | entropy R² |
|---|---|
| no examples at all (k=0) | 0.262 |
| 10 full examples | 0.176 |
| 10 shortened examples | 0.193 |
| 30 full | 0.144 |
| 30 shortened | 0.194 |
| 93 full | 0.158 |
| 93 shortened | 0.171 |
| 400 shortened | 0.156 |

**Shortened scores higher in 5 of the 6 same-k comparisons** (3 values of k, 2 targets):

| k | entropy, full | entropy, short | remaining length, full | remaining length, short |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 10 | 0.176 | 0.193 | -0.162 | -0.060 (significant, +0.102) |
| 30 | 0.144 | 0.194 | -0.006 | +0.014 |
| 93 | 0.158 | 0.171 | +0.041 | +0.039 |

Only one of the six is significant on its own, and 5 of 6 in the same direction is not significant
by a sign test either, so **call it suggestive, not established.**

**What it does not change:** the best shortened setting reaches entropy 0.194, against 0.262 with
no examples at all and 0.309 for our probe. Shortening makes a bad setting slightly less bad. It
does not make in-context learning work.

**Correction to note:** an earlier draft of these notes said shortening destroyed the
remaining-length prediction. That was backwards: it improves it, significantly at k=10. The real
remaining-length story is that **any** examples collapse it, from 0.283 with none to about zero
with 10 or more.

Source: `runs/goal2/gpqa25/sweep/_icl/`, raw files `icl/raw/gemma4_31b_off_s2_k*`.
Job 4942955, 1 hour 11 minutes on 2 GPUs, zero parse failures.

**If he pushes:** the headline is that the prompt being too long was never the problem. Giving the
model more examples makes it worse, whether those examples are long or short. Shortening recovers a
little of what the long examples cost, which is consistent with long examples crowding out the
query, but not enough to matter.

## Ask 2. Frontier model (slide 7)

**What he asked for:** try the in-context setup with a frontier model rather than a small local one.

**What we did:** GPT-6 Luna, reasoning effort high, 8 prompt variants, on the full 1,178-row sample.
9,424 API calls, $18, zero parse failures.

**Result, mean over the 10% to 90% cut points, best setting (k=0):**

| target | GPT-6 Luna | our probe | difference | significant |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| entropy | 0.294 | 0.309 | -0.015 | no |
| P(mode) | 0.233 | 0.261 | -0.028 | no |
| remaining length | 0.455 | 0.563 | -0.107 | **yes** |
| length spread | 0.122 | 0.328 | -0.206 | **yes** |
| commitment | 0.718 | 0.727 | -0.008 | no |

**Read it as:** a frontier model told the task in words matches us on the answer-distribution
targets, and loses badly on the two length targets.

**The more interesting finding: more examples make it worse.** k=0 beats k=50 beats k=10. Gemma does
exactly the same thing locally, where k=0 scores 0.262 and 93 examples collapses to 0.158. Two
unrelated model families, same shape.

A caution worth volunteering: these numbers are calibrated. The models' raw guesses are on the wrong
scale, so for each fold we fit a straight line from predicted to actual using the other folds, then
apply it. Without that, the raw R² is below zero almost everywhere. That is standard, but say it
before he finds it.

Source: `runs/goal2/gpqa25/icl/`, comparison in `sweep/_icl/`.

## Ask 3. Remove the checkpoint location (slide 8)

**What he asked for:** the prompt tells the model how far through the chain the cut is. Take that
away.

**What we found first:** we already had a version that hid it, but it only hid it on the examples,
not on the query. So it was never a real test. We built a proper one that hides it everywhere.

**Result:**

| target | effect of hiding it |
|---|---|
| entropy, P(mode), commitment | no change at any k |
| remaining length | R2 falls by 0.119 / 0.239 / 0.166 at k = 0 / 10 / 50, significant at every k |
| length spread | R2 falls by 0.059 / 0.095 / 0.071 at k = 0 / 10 / 50, significant at every k |

**Why this is a good result rather than a boring one:** it shows the model actually reads and uses
the depth label, but only where depth is genuinely informative. Knowing you are 90% through tells
you a lot about how much is left and nothing about which answer you will give. If hiding it had
changed nothing at all, that would have suggested the model was ignoring the prompt.

## Asks 5, 6 and 9. Different family, random forest grid, embeddings (slides 9 and 10)

These three land on one comparison, so we ran everything through identical scoring: 22 approaches,
same rows, same folds, same error bars.

**What is in it:**

- Our probe, plus the two text baselines
- 4 models from other families: Qwen2.5-Instruct, Qwen2.5-Math, OLMo-3-7B, Mistral-7B
- 5 sentence embedders: Qwen3-Embedding-8B, F2LLM-v2-8B, UltraBERT, BGE-M3, Granite
- 7 probe types: two MLP sizes, two random forests, gradient boosting, two PCA variants
- 3 layer choices: layer 14, layers 13 to 15, layers 20 to 22

**Result: only one of the 21 alternatives beats our probe, and barely.** Layers 20 to 22 stacked
together gives +0.005 on entropy.

**Ties (no significant difference on any target):** Qwen2.5-Instruct, Qwen2.5-Math, OLMo-3.

**Everything else is significantly worse.**

### Ask 5 in detail: the other-family result is the uncomfortable one

Take a completely different model, one that never produced these chains, feed it the same text, read
its hidden state, and it predicts just as well as the model that actually wrote the chain.

OLMo-3-7B ties us on all five targets, with differences under 0.008. OLMo-3 shares no training
lineage with our model at all.

**What this means:** the signal is mostly "what does this text look like", not "what is this
particular model about to do". That weakens the most exciting reading of the project. It is in the
deck honestly because he will spot it otherwise, and because it connects directly to the
preliminary finding on slide 16.

Mistral-7B is slightly below us (entropy -0.018, significant) but still far above the text
baselines.

### Ask 6 in detail: random forests

We ran a grid and then went further and asked why they lose.

- Best random forest: entropy 0.235 against our 0.289.
- Gradient boosting is the best tree model at 0.258, still below.
- Regularising the forest until training and test agree only lowers the test score. It does not
  close the gap.
- **The reason is structural, not tuning.** Our linear probe spreads its weight across roughly
  1,600 of 3,584 dimensions. Trees split one dimension at a time, so a broad spread of small
  contributions is exactly what they are bad at. We confirmed this by making up a known linear
  function on the same inputs with no noise at all: the forest scored 0.68 where ridge scored 0.92.

Source: `sweep/4c_rf_*`, `sweep/rf_reg/`, `sweep/_round2/r3_rf/`.

### Ask 9 in detail: embeddings

Five embedders, including ones trained for long documents.

The best of them, UltraBERT, reaches entropy 0.260. That is not significantly different from our
0.289, but it is also not significantly different from TF-IDF. **So the best sentence embedder only
gets you to where plain word counting already was.** The rest are clearly worse. Granite is below
TF-IDF on everything.

Source: `sweep/feat_*`, `docs/embedders.md`.

## Ask 7. Is LoRA working? (slide 11)

**What he asked for:** check whether LoRA is actually doing anything.

**Background:** LoRA fine-tunes the model slightly while training the probe, instead of freezing it.
If the frozen hidden state is missing something, this should recover it.

**First answer looked bad:** LoRA plus its own prediction head scored well below plain ridge.

**We did not stop there.** We took the same prediction head and the same training recipe, and
trained it on the frozen stored states with no LoRA at all. It reproduced the same deficit. **So the
problem was the head's training recipe, not LoRA.**

Then we did the clean version: train the LoRA, save the adapted hidden states, and fit ordinary
ridge on those.

| cut point | ridge on LoRA states | ridge on stored states | difference |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10% | 0.281 | 0.278 | +0.003, not significant |
| 30% | 0.307 | 0.295 | +0.012, significant |
| 50% | 0.309 | 0.298 | +0.012, significant |
| 70% | 0.312 | 0.302 | +0.009, significant |
| 90% | 0.291 | 0.270 | +0.021, significant |

(entropy; the other targets follow the same pattern)

**Answer: yes, LoRA works, and it gives a small real gain, growing with depth.** About 3 to 7
percent relative. 12 GPU-hours total.

A sanity check we ran and can quote: the same pipeline applied to the unmodified stored states
reproduces the original ridge numbers exactly, difference 0.000. So the gain is from LoRA, not from
a change in the fitting code.

Source: `runs/goal2/gpqa25/lora/`, CIs in `lora/ci_f0.*.csv`.

# Part 4. The two things we looked into ourselves

These were not asked for. They came out of round 4.

## Point 1. Is our training data too similar? (slides 12 and 13)

**The worry:** each question has 25 chains. If those 25 are near-copies of each other, the probe has
far less real information than the row count suggests, and the whole project might be measuring
noise.

**Two independent checks say no.**

### Check one: how alike are the chains, actually

We measure whether two chains of the same question look more alike than two chains of different
questions, and express it as a separation score where 0.5 means no difference and 1.0 means
perfectly distinguishable.

| what we measured | score |
|---|---|
| activations at the 10% cut | 0.80 |
| activations at the 90% cut | 0.71 |
| the raw text, as word counts | **1.000** |

So yes, chains of a question cluster, but not completely. And the striking line is the last one: the
raw text is at 1.000, perfectly clustered. **The activations are considerably less question-clumped
than the text is.**

### Check two: does more chains still help

Retrain using only m chains per question, test on the same held-out rows throughout. Entropy at the
50% cut:

| chains per question | R² |
|---|---|
| 1 | 0.152 |
| 2 | 0.203 |
| 3 | 0.228 |
| 5 | 0.240 |
| 10 | 0.278 |
| 25 | 0.298 |

Still rising at 25, no plateau. **If the chains were near-duplicates this curve would have gone
flat.** It does not.

### But the rollouts are badly spent

Same question from the other direction: at a fixed number of training rows, is it better to have
many questions with few chains each, or few questions with many chains?

At about 300 rows:

| layout | entropy R² |
|---|---|
| 12 questions x 25 chains | 0.063 |
| 30 questions x 10 chains | 0.168 |
| 60 questions x 5 chains | 0.182 |
| **150 questions x 2 chains** | **0.202** |

At about 750 rows:

| layout | entropy R² |
|---|---|
| 30 questions x 25 chains | 0.196 |
| 75 questions x 10 chains | 0.227 |
| **150 questions x 5 chains** | **0.243** |

**The headline: 150 questions with 2 chains each (300 rows) matches 30 questions with 25 chains
(744 rows).** Same quality for 2.5 times fewer rollouts, which is the expensive part.

**Recommendation if we collect more data: many more questions, 2 to 5 chains each.** This is the one
actionable result in the whole deck.

Source: `runs/goal2/gpqa25/sweep/_redundancy/`.

## Point 2. Why is a linear probe still the best? (slides 14 and 15)

**The background:** for weeks our answer was "we tried MLPs, random forests, gradient boosting and
LoRA, and plain linear ridge beat all of them". That is a suspicious result. It usually means
something is wrong with how the alternatives were trained.

**It was.** We checked our own training code against a case where we know the right answer: fit a
linear model with the optimiser, and compare to the exact closed-form solution.

Over 80 fits:

- A proper optimiser (L-BFGS) reproduces the exact answer at a similarity of **1.0000**.
- **Our training recipe sat at 0.865 on average, and as low as 0.630.**

So our recipe was not even finding the linear solution it should have matched, let alone beating it.
That cost between 0.008 and 0.025 R², which is the size of the entire effect we were looking for.
**Every previous MLP and LoRA-head result was measuring our own bug.**

### What we did about it

Fixed the recipe, then settled the question a way that removes the optimiser from the picture
entirely: **kernel ridge regression**. A degree-2 kernel fits all pairwise products of the inputs,
and it has an exact closed-form solution, so there is no training loop to get wrong. If curved
structure exists, this finds it.

**Result: the ceiling depends on the target.**

| target | gain from allowing curvature | significant |
|---|---|---|
| remaining length | +0.0081 | **yes** |
| length spread | +0.0027 | no (except at 90%) |
| P(mode) | +0.0024 | no |
| commitment | +0.0007 | no |
| entropy | -0.0035 | significantly **worse** |

**So three of our five targets are genuinely linear-ceilinged.** It is not that we failed to find
the curve. There is no curve to find.

With the recipe fixed, a carefully built MLP does finally beat ridge on remaining length, by +0.0039
(interval +0.0018 to +0.0063). It needed all three of: cross-fitted residuals, a square activation,
and averaging over 5 random seeds. A single seed is not significant at any cut point.

### Two more pieces of evidence for "there is no headroom"

**The probe already throws most of itself away.** Ridge uses an effective 440 of 3,584 directions,
about 12%. It is choosing to be simple.

**Forcing more capacity does not help.** We took an MLP and forced it to actually use its non-linear
part. Training score 0.9995, test score 0.300, against 0.548 for the plain linear model on the same
rows. It memorised the training set and got worse at the real task.

**What to say if he asks "so should we try architecture X":** we would rather not. The closed-form
kernel establishes the ceiling without needing to trust any training code, and on three of five
targets the ceiling is zero.

Source: `runs/goal2/gpqa25/sweep/_mlpgap/`.

# Part 5. The preliminary finding (slide 16)

**This is the one we want his read on. It is marked preliminary on the slide on purpose.**

## What we did

Our R² mixes two different abilities:

1. Telling **which questions** will produce uncertain answers (some questions are just harder)
2. Telling **which chain of a given question** will produce uncertain answers

Those are very different claims. The second is the interesting one: it would mean the model knows
where this particular line of reasoning is heading. The first is closer to "this question is hard",
which you might guess from the question alone.

We split the score into those two parts.

## What we found

| cut point | across questions | within one question |
|---|---|---|
| 10% | 0.398 | -0.265 |
| 30% | 0.463 | -0.125 |
| 50% | 0.527 | -0.057 |
| 70% | 0.563 | +0.013 |
| 90% | 0.542 | +0.063 |

(entropy. Remaining length shows the same shape.)

**Across questions it is strong. Within a question it is near zero, and negative early on.**
Negative means worse than just guessing that question's average.

## What we checked before believing it

- **Is it just noise?** No. Our labels come from 25 finishes, so they are reasonably precise. Noise
  accounts for only 6% to 45% of the within-question variation, so there is real signal there to
  find. We reach 0.3% to 12% of what is reachable.
- **Can we fix it by training differently?** We tried training directly on question-adjusted targets.
  It did not recover it, and on some targets made it worse.
- **Does the text do better?** No. TF-IDF is about the same or better within a question. So this is
  not an activations failure specifically.

## Why it matters

It does not change a single published number. **It changes the sentence the paper is built around.**

The strong claim, "the model is tracking where this chain is heading", is not supported.

The supported claim is: **activations beat text at judging which questions will produce uncertain
rollouts. They do not beat text at judging which chain of a given question will.**

It also explains the other-family result from ask 5. If the signal is mostly question difficulty,
then of course another model reading the same question gets it too.

Nobody else in this literature splits their R² this way. That could be a contribution in itself.

## The experiment that would settle it

Probe the **0% cut point**: the question as given, before any reasoning has been written.

- Prefix length is constant there, so that confound disappears completely
- One row per question, so the probe can only read question difficulty
- Whatever it scores is a direct measurement of the difficulty floor rather than an inference

The data has been ready since early October. It needs about 30 lines of code and a few CPU-hours.
A published NeurIPS 2025 paper probes exactly this position, so our number would be directly
comparable.

**Cheap companion:** predict each question's average label from the question text alone, no chain at
all. If that recovers most of our R², the question-difficulty story is settled beyond argument.

## Ask 4. GEPA prompt optimization

His description was: vary the prompt from training samples, get an optimal set of training examples,
then test. Two separable things:

- **Instruction optimization:** rewrite the task description automatically against a held-out score.
- **Example selection:** choose which k examples go into the prompt, rather than our current

**The thing to raise:** our own results undercut example selection. More examples make performance

to reverse that, or whether instruction optimization is the real target.

**Second thing to settle:** what we optimize against. Tuning prompts against our fold structure
risks fitting the test set unless we hold out a further split.

# Part 7. Questions he might ask, and the answers

**"Is 0.29 good?"**
It is clearly above chance and above both text baselines. The only directly comparable published
number is DyCon (ICML 2026), which reports 0.501 on remaining length using our exact model; we get
0.524. Their split does not group by problem, so theirs is an upper bound.

**"Why not just pool all the cut points?"**
Because pooled, the trivial "how long is the prefix" baseline gets 0.718 and we get 0.716. Pooling
measures how well you know your position in the chain, not what we are trying to measure.

**"How do I know this is not just overfitting?"**
Split is by question, with fixed folds. Ridge's training score is 0.50 against 0.29 test, which is a
real gap but it is the best test score of anything we tried. The forest was worse: 0.82 training
against 0.24 test.

**"Why layer 21?"**
Inherited from the original setup. We have since tested layers 13 to 15, 14, and 20 to 22. Only 20
to 22 stacked beats it, by 0.005. Mid-depth layers are worse. 24 of 28 layers are still untested,
which is a fair criticism.

**"Why only one model and one dataset?"**
Fair. Everything is GPQA-Diamond and R1-Distill-Qwen-7B. The allocation result in Part 4 says what
a second dataset should look like: many more questions, few chains each.

**"Are your error bars honest?"**
Question-level bootstrap, 2,000 draws, paired differences. Roughly plus or minus 0.05 for comparing
two different feature sets. We report ties as ties.

**"What is the weakest part of this?"**
Two things, and volunteer them: another model's activations do just as well as our own model's, and
the score is mostly question difficulty rather than chain direction. Both are on the slides.
